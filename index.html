Article 53. A decision aid.

I wanted to understand Article 53 as objectively as I could. So I read the warrant language, the substitute motions, ARB materials, plan drawings, assessor records, and the relevant zoning and code constraints. This page is my working decision aid. I am sharing it in case it is useful to other Town Meeting Members weighing the same question.

Article 53 is described as an administrative clarification, but the substitute motions would make a substantive policy choice: how to measure the 60% ground-floor commercial requirement for the MBTA-C mixed-use bonus.

Quick conclusions

  • The current ARB approach already produces real storefronts. In the stress-test example, the proposed commercial space is 1,467 sf, a normal Broadway-scale size.
  • The Cullinane amendment can make the bonus hard to use on some Broadway lots. In the example below, the project already has 1,467 sf of storefront, a normal Broadway-scale space that fits all four allowed bonus uses. Cullinane asks for another 574 sf on top of that, but there is no obvious place to put it without taking away parking, access, utilities, bike storage, or required building space. The result may be no bonus storefront at all, and less new tax revenue.
  • The Macaluso amendment is softer, but not neutral. It works on this stress-test lot, but can still bind on other constrained layouts.
  • The tradeoff is not just commercial square footage. If the bonus is skipped, Arlington can lose a bonus floor of housing, affordable units, storefront activation, and tax base.
  • The revenue tradeoff matters right now. Arlington just passed an operating override, and this Town Meeting already approved fee increases under an earlier article. We should be careful about making private corridor investment harder when it can add future tax base.
  • This is a stress test, not every Broadway project. 259 Broadway and the newer 126-128 Broadway plan appear to pass under Cullinane; withdrawn Docket #3862 shows where the rule can break down.

What each rule actually delivers.

I used the withdrawn 126 Broadway application, Docket #3862, as the stress-test lot for this analysis: about 3,400 sf of ground-floor footprint, 14 homes including 3 affordable homes, 1,467 sf of proposed commercial space, and podium parking. For the tax-base estimate, I use $319 per gross square foot, the lower of the two recent new-construction corridor comps shown later.

Why this example?

This uses the withdrawn 126 Broadway podium-garage plan because it is the useful stress test. It is also the docket where this interpretation question came up and led the ARB to its current practice. The site is typical of Broadway parcels where the ground floor may need to fit commercial space, access, utilities, and podium parking under the upper floors. The approved 259 Broadway plan and the newer 126-128 Broadway plan appear to pass even under the Cullinane language, so they do not show the tradeoff. The policy question is what happens to the Broadway lots where parking and building support functions sit under the upper floors.

Does the Cullinane amendment actually deliver a bigger storefront?

The Comment section of the Cullinane/Slotnick substitute says the motion would clarify the calculation and "re-affirm the agreed-upon proportion" of ground-floor commercial use.

On this podium-garage Broadway model, small parcel, ground-floor open garage with upper floors above, the amendment expands the denominator from the enclosed ground floor to the entire footprint including the garage. The storefront would have to grow by ~574 sf, from the 1,467 sf proposed under current ARB practice to about 2,041 sf under the Cullinane/Slotnick calculation. There are five places that area could come from. Use the plan below as a test: try to find where the missing 574 sf can come from, then see what the bylaw and building code actually require.

Verdict

The amendment defeats its own stated goal.

The Comment section of the Cullinane/Slotnick substitute says the motion would clarify the calculation and "re-affirm the agreed-upon proportion" of ground-floor commercial use. But on a podium-garage Broadway lot like this model, that proportion is difficult to satisfy. Every place the required +574 sf could come from is locked by site plan review, the parking bylaw, the building code, or state accessibility law. The likely result on a podium-parking Broadway lot is not the ~2,040 sf storefront the amendment demands. It is a project that cannot use the commercial bonus at all. Without qualifying commercial, the bonus doesn't apply: the project shrinks, redesigns, or doesn't get built. That means no ~$930K bonus-floor assessed value, no ~$10K / year bonus-floor tax revenue, and one fewer floor of housing. If the whole project is not financially or physically workable, the potential loss scales to $30K-$60K per year in tax revenue, per project.

A useful counter-example

Why some Broadway projects already pass even Cullinane.

A few Broadway projects put parking outdoors, behind the building rather than in an open ground-floor garage. On those projects no upper floor projects over the parking, so even Cullinane's own text, "the two-dimensional area of the surface that the structure occupies … projected onto the ground plane, including cantilevered portions", doesn't pull the parking into the 60% denominator. Commercial as a share of enclosed ground floor stays at ~60% and the project complies under all three rules.

Cullinane's amendment isn't really about the 60% target, it's about which design choices count toward it. Open ground-floor garages have been an accepted design for mixed-use buildings throughout the corridor; Cullinane's reading places that specific layout under a stricter test. ARB practice keeps the 60% target while staying neutral on design.

What businesses can actually open in 1,467 sf?

Proponents argue Article 53 is about getting "real" storefronts instead of token commercial space. But MBTA-C explicitly limits the bonus to four use categories. 1,467 sf already fits every one of them, measured against industry averages and Massachusetts state licensing requirements, not against vibes.

Bottom line

Many existing Broadway storefronts are at or below 1,467 sf. MBTA-C only permits four bonus categories, and the larger formats (full fast-casual, full c-store, big-box) are not permitted bonus uses at all. Forcing more square footage doesn't change which businesses can locate there, it only changes whether the bonus floor gets built. As the simulation above shows, on podium-parking Broadway lots like this one, the Cullinane amendment can make the commercial bonus impractical rather than producing a larger storefront.

Important caveat

What about the Macaluso amendment?

Macaluso is a softer version of Cullinane. On the stress-test lot modeled here it lands in the same place as current ARB practice, so the project can still deliver the 1,467 sf storefront, the homes, and the added tax revenue. But on other Broadway lots the math can tip the same way as Cullinane and make those outcomes less likely.

Net: current ARB practice is the rule most likely to deliver actual commercial space and new tax revenue across Broadway lots.

Where I land.

Where I come out

I am not persuaded that either amendment improves the bylaw.

The ARB recommended no action. Based on this review, current practice appears more likely to produce the mix of outcomes the bylaw is trying to encourage: usable storefronts, housing, affordable units, and added tax base on constrained Broadway lots.

What weighs most for me
  • 1,467 sf already fits every commercial use MBTA-C permits as a bonus tenant.
  • The Cullinane amendment may not deliver more; on podium-garage lots, the likely places to add space are already doing necessary work.
  • If the bonus is not used, the town can lose housing, affordable units, storefront activation, and future tax revenue.
  • The Macaluso amendment is better than Cullinane, but it still adds a new test that can matter on other constrained layouts.
How I am using this
  • To separate the stated goal from the practical design effect.
  • To compare the three rules against the same stress-test lot.
  • To check whether the added commercial requirement produces an actual storefront or makes the bonus harder to use.

Model assumptions

  • Broadway podium-garage stress-test lot modeled on the withdrawn Docket #3862 application and the local plan PDFs: 5,407 sf parcel, 5-story podium design, ~3,400 sf ground-floor footprint, 1,817 sf enclosed (FAR), 1,467 sf proposed commercial, 5 ground-floor garage spaces, 14 homes (3 affordable).
  • Under current ARB practice (enclosed denominator only): 1,467 / 1,817 = 80.7%, passes 60%.
  • Under Cullinane (full footprint including garage under upper floors): 1,467 / 3,401 = 43.1%, fails 60%; required commercial = 60% × 3,401 = 2,041 sf → +574 sf gap.
  • Bonus floor on the stress-test lot: ~2,920 sf (5th story). At $319 / gsf benchmark: ~$930K assessed value, ~$10K / yr in tax revenue.
  • Why not use 259 Broadway or new 126-128 Broadway for the simulation? Those plan types appear to pass under Cullinane because their parking/design does not create the same denominator problem. They are useful pass-case examples, but 126 Broadway is the stress-test case.
